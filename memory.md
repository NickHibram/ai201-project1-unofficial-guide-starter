## Question
What are examples of Japanese instruments

## Answer
According to **A Complete History of Music**, a Japanese clarinet‑type instrument called the *Hichi‑riki* is mentioned, and the text also notes the *Sho* (similar to the Chinese Sheng) and the national instrument, the *Koto*.  

According to **Musical Instruments, Historic, Rare and Unique**, the *Siamisen* is described as a common Japanese stringed instrument, and the *Biwa* and *Kokiu* are also identified as Japanese instruments.

## Question
How do you build a bagpipe

## Answer
According to **The Highland bagpipe**, the instrument is assembled from several distinct parts. The bag itself is made of sheepskin and has five pieces of turned wood called stocks securely fastened to it; these stocks receive the ends of the chanter, the mouth‑piece, and the drones. The chanter reed is constructed from two pieces of Spanish cane placed side by side, with their tops worked to a fine edge and their bottoms tied with fine hemp to a small metal tube. The blowpipe includes a valve on its lower end that prevents wind from returning to the player’s mouth.  

The drones are fitted with metal linings where there is friction in the tuning slide. The largest drone has two tuning slides, while the others have one each, and the drones are interchangeable so the big drone can be placed in either the right or left stock to suit a right‑ or left‑handed player.  

In addition, the overall layout consists of a valved tube leading from the mouth to an airtight bag that has four other openings: three large ones for the bases of the three fixed long tubes (the drones) and a smaller one for the chanter, which is held in the hands while the drones rest on the shoulder.

## Question
What does it sound like


## Answer
According to **The Highland bagpipe**, the instrument’s tone is portrayed as harsh, imperfect, and untunable, producing a “hideous yell” that grunts, howls, screams, and screeches like a pig or a portable screech‑owl. It is likened to a “devil machine” that tears at the listener’s nerves, almost waking the dead. Another passage likens sermons to Scottish bagpipes, saying they “sound very well when one doesn’t hear them,” underscoring the unpleasantness of its sound. Footnote 13 further emphasizes its harshness, describing the experience of hearing it as a “general deliquium” that leaves listeners feeling indurated and desensitized.
